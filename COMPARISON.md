I compared three methods using the same 98 supplied policies and 30 fixed questions: rules-based keyword search, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite given all policies, and the same model given five policies selected by a saved Gemini embedding index. Rules returned 30 results, while each AI method returned 21 results and had nine unresolved service errors. AI-assisted review judged 17/30 rules answers and 20/21 successful answers from each AI method correct; these are different successful subsets, not proof of equal performance. The rules method added no unsupported claims, although its exact extracts often missed the question. Each AI method made one unsupported claim among 21 answers (4.8%): it inferred that exactly $500 never requires Finance approval, although the policy only says expenses over $500 require approval. Successful-response medians were 2.13 ms for rules, 25.53 seconds for full context and 27.05 seconds for vector retrieval. Mean generation-token use was 0, 5,400 and 536 respectively. These times mix sequential and concurrent requests and exclude failed attempts; they do not establish a controlled speed ranking. Embedding token usage was not reported, and preparing the 98-vector index took 139.9 seconds including deliberate pacing.

For this small database, I prefer the full-context LLM as the simplest starting point: all policies fit in the prompt, and it avoids maintaining an index or excluding evidence before generation. This is a practical design judgement, not a measured quality victory. Vector retrieval reduced reported generation tokens by about 90% and is attractive as the database grows, but embedding work and retrieval omissions must also be considered; here it did not eliminate unsupported inference or service failures. Rules remain a transparent, fast, zero-generation-token fallback, with weaker handling of paraphrases, missing details and combined policies. A valid citation is insufficient to guarantee a supported answer, so the app shows policy quotes and limitations and the Slack bot suppresses malformed citations. The review is AI-assisted and needs student verification; the small, partly developmental test set, different successful subsets, retained retries and provider outages limit generalization. The website exposes individual answers, source policies, timing, token metadata, review notes and failures so these conclusions can be checked.
